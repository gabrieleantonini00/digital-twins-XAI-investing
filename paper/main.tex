\begin{abstract}
\noindent This computational experiment examines whether the disclosure of algorithmic explanations changes willingness to invest in an AI-managed fund, and whether this association depends on market context and an inferred financial-literacy proxy. Five hundred distinct personas were sampled without replacement from Twin-2K-500 and randomly assigned to a balanced $2\times2$ between-persona design: bull versus bear market and standard versus explainable AI, with 125 personas per condition. OpenAI GPT-5.6 Sol generated one investment response per persona, using medium reasoning effort and temperature 1. Baseline characteristics were assessed separately before exposure to the experimental scenario. A cumulative logit model retained the numerical literacy proxy, while OLS with HC3 standard errors and a median-split aligned rank transform analysis served as sensitivity checks. \AbstractFinding\ These results characterize the behavior of this particular simulation engine and do not establish effects on human investors or algorithm aversion relative to a human advisor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Investment decisions increasingly involve automated systems whose internal reasoning may be difficult for users to inspect. Research on algorithm aversion has shown that people can become reluctant to rely on algorithms after observing their errors, even when algorithmic performance remains comparatively strong \citep{dietvorst2015}. Explainability offers one possible way to communicate how such systems reach decisions. The present study examines a narrower, directly measurable question: how does the description of an explanation dashboard affect willingness to invest in an AI-managed fund under different market conditions?

The research question is: \textit{Does the effect of algorithmic transparency on simulated willingness to invest vary jointly with market context and baseline financial literacy?} Market downturns may increase demand for understandable risk information; alternatively, explicit explanations could make risks more salient and discourage investment. A person's financial knowledge may also shape the interpretation of an explanation. These competing mechanisms make it inappropriate to treat greater transparency as necessarily beneficial in every setting.

The motivating expectations are that explanations increase willingness to invest, particularly among personas with lower financial literacy, and that this pattern is more pronounced in bear markets. They are examined within the continuous three-way interaction and the planned conditional contrasts. These expectations were informed by the earlier exploratory pilot. The new analysis plan was fixed locally before collection of the new investment outcomes; this is not a claim of public preregistration.

The study uses persona-conditioned LLM responses to obtain a controlled computational experiment. The underlying profiles come from survey-based persona information, but the investment outcomes are newly generated by the model. The contribution is therefore a reproducible test of \textit{simulated} investment intentions, with a larger and more transparently documented sample than the earlier 100-persona pilot. It is not a substitute for a human-subject replication.

\section{Experimental Design and Data Collection}
\subsection{Sampling and randomization}
The source is the \texttt{full\_persona} configuration of Twin-2K-500, which provides profiles derived from a multiwave survey of 2,058 US participants \citep{twin2025}. The exact source revision is recorded in the replication files. The simulation uses the supplied \texttt{persona\_summary}; it does not generate new identities or use the full questionnaire text.

Five hundred profiles were sampled without replacement using seed 42. This fixed sample size was chosen to broaden profile coverage within the computational budget; it was not determined by a significance-based stopping rule or a formal prospective power target. The pipeline required both 500 unique participant IDs and 500 unique persona summaries. Each profile was assigned to one of four combinations of market context and transparency using a seeded shuffle of a balanced allocation list. There are exactly 125 profiles per randomized condition. Financial literacy is an inferred baseline moderator; it is not a randomized third treatment. Consequently, the design is a randomized $2\times2$ experiment with a moderator, rather than eight independently randomized treatment cells.

\subsection{Two separate measurement stages}
The baseline stage assessed each summary without revealing the assigned scenario. Financial literacy and technological familiarity were estimated on integer scales from 1 to 7. The prompt requested direct evidence where available and explicitly prohibited assigning high or low values solely from age, gender, or education. An intermediate score of 4 was permitted when evidence was insufficient. Age, gender, and education were extracted only when explicitly stated; unknown demographics remained missing.

These financial-literacy and technological-familiarity values are \textbf{LLM-inferred proxies}, not observed financial-knowledge test scores. In particular, a score of 4 can reflect either intermediate inferred knowledge or insufficient profile information. This limits the interpretation of literacy moderation. The baseline stage prevents exposure of the proxy assessment to the experimental scenario, but it does not validate that proxy against human measurements.

In the outcome stage, a new request contained the same persona summary, its fixed baseline characterization, and one assigned vignette. The person evaluated a new investment fund completely managed by an AI algorithm. Willingness to invest was measured as a single integer from 1 (\textit{Absolutely not willing}) to 7 (\textit{Extremely willing}). Each persona contributed one investment outcome. No temperature comparison or repeated outcome sampling was conducted, and the older Groq/Llama observations were not pooled with the new data.

\subsection{Experimental manipulations}
The bull-market description stated that the economy was thriving, with consistent growth, high consumer confidence, and strong positive trends over the previous 12 months. The bear-market description stated that the economy was in a severe downturn, with high volatility, widespread financial losses, and significant uncertainty over the previous 12 months.

The standard-AI condition provided only performance KPIs, with proprietary and hidden internal decision logic. The XAI condition described a dashboard displaying decision rationales, feature weights, and risk assessments. These are descriptions of disclosure availability, not exposure to a working explanation system. No human-advisor condition, realized investment returns, fees, or performance guarantees were supplied.

\subsection{Model configuration and computational controls}
All requests used OpenAI \texttt{gpt-5.6-sol}, reasoning effort \texttt{medium}, temperature 1, low output verbosity, and a maximum of 1,536 output tokens including reasoning. No \texttt{top\_p} override was supplied. A technical compatibility request contained no persona and was not part of the research sample. The API accepted the chosen reasoning and temperature combination. The model identifier and returned usage are recorded in the audit files; the provider did not expose a dated snapshot identifier in the selected alias.

Strict JSON schemas constrained the requested response fields. The two stages were executed through the Batch API, which offers asynchronous processing at reduced rates \citep{openaiBatch}. To respect the account's queued-token limit, each stage was transported as sequential lots of 200, 200, and 100. The initial 500-request job was rejected during validation with no processed requests. Splitting transport did not alter sample membership, randomization, prompts, or model settings.

Responses were reconciled by unique request ID and participant ID, independently of output-file order. The pipeline checked types, ranges, duplicate IDs, missing results, and response completion. It required all 500 valid baseline assessments and all 500 valid investment outcomes before exporting the final dataset. It did not automatically redraw failed or incomplete investment responses. Input files, source profiles, credentials, and raw API responses are kept outside the public replication artifacts. The public audit includes sample counts, configuration, checksums, batch identifiers, and token usage.

\section{Analysis Plan}
\subsection{Primary ordinal model}
Because the outcome is an ordered seven-category response, the primary analysis uses a cumulative logit model \citep{christensen}. Let $M_i=1$ denote a bull market, $T_i=1$ denote XAI, and $L_i$ denote the financial-literacy proxy centered at 4. The model is
\begin{align}
\operatorname{logit}\{P(Y_i\leq k)\}&=\theta_k-\eta_i,\\
\eta_i&=\beta_M M_i+\beta_T T_i+\beta_L L_i\\
&\quad+\beta_{MT}M_iT_i+\beta_{ML}M_iL_i+\beta_{TL}T_iL_i
+\beta_{MTL}M_iT_iL_i .
\end{align}
Positive location coefficients correspond to greater odds of reporting a higher willingness category. The literacy score is entered numerically with a linear effect on the log-odds scale. This avoids an arbitrary primary median split, while retaining assumptions about equal increments in the proxy and linearity that require caution.

The primary test compares this full model with the hierarchical model containing all main effects and two-way interactions, using a likelihood-ratio test of the three-way interaction. Model convergence and proportional-odds diagnostics are reported. Numerical instability or failure of the proportional-odds assumption limits interpretation; sensitivity analyses do not automatically repair those limitations.

\subsection{Planned conditional comparisons and sensitivity checks}
Six planned comparisons estimate XAI versus standard AI at proxy values 2, 4, and 6 within each market. The six $p$-values are adjusted together using Holm's procedure. The reported 95\% confidence intervals are pointwise Wald intervals, not simultaneous intervals. Contrasts outside the observed literacy range of either compared cell are flagged as extrapolations.

Two sensitivity analyses accompany the primary model. First, an OLS regression uses the same continuous-moderator interaction, with HC3 standard errors. Second, a legacy-style ART analysis uses low/high literacy groups defined relative to the sample median \citep{wobbrock2011}. This median split is a sensitivity analysis rather than the primary operationalization; ties can create unequal subgroup sizes. ART results concern aligned ranks and are not directly comparable to ordinal odds ratios. Raw-score normality tests were not used to select the primary model. Full ART-C pairwise comparisons are supplied as supplementary sensitivity output \citep{elkin2021}.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
Random assignment permits a controlled comparison of vignette descriptions within this simulation. Nevertheless, all investment outcomes come from a single model configuration. A larger number of profiles improves precision within that engine; it does not remove shared model biases or establish population-level human effects. The supplied persona summaries may omit relevant investing experience and compress or distort the original survey information. The inferred literacy proxy is especially consequential: limited variation, neutral scores assigned for missing evidence, and dependence on the same model family can all affect estimated moderation.

The XAI vignette changes the stated availability and type of information, so its effect cannot be separated from the effect of additional risk information or the favorable framing of an explanation dashboard. No actual explanations, portfolio choices, stakes, or realized outcomes were observed. The absence of a human-advisor comparison also prevents identification of algorithm aversion as a relative preference for human advice. Willingness to invest, trust, and algorithm aversion should therefore not be treated as interchangeable outcomes.

The earlier 100-persona pilot and the present study differ in sample, model, temperature, measurement separation, and analysis. Any change in findings cannot be causally attributed to a single one of those changes. The expanded study should be judged on its own estimates and uncertainty, rather than on whether it reproduces the pilot's significant results. Human-subject validation and direct baseline financial-knowledge measures remain necessary before drawing practical conclusions about investors.

\section{Prompt Structure}
The baseline request receives only the persona summary and asks for inferred literacy and technology-familiarity scores, plus explicitly stated demographic characteristics. It contains no assigned market or transparency information. The outcome request receives a system instruction to simulate the described person, the same summary, the fixed baseline JSON, and one market/transparency scenario. It asks only for the integer willingness response and does not request a rationale. Both prompts instruct the model to treat profile contents as data rather than instructions. The exact executable prompts and JSON schemas are versioned in the simulation script.
